I've had two summers as a machine learning research intern, and the second went far better than the first. Nothing in this post is about a particular algorithm; it's the working habits I wish someone had handed me on day one.

## In the first week

- **Get your environment working before anything else.** A machine you can trust, with the tools and access you need, is worth a couple of days of patience. I learned to ask for access early instead of waiting for someone to notice.
- **Find out what "done" means.** For a research problem, that could be a number, a demo, a write-up, or code that runs in the team's system. Those are very different weeks of work.
- **Learn the existing system before rebuilding it.** Sitting down with the people who built the current version, and letting them walk me through how it works, saved me from reinventing (badly) things they'd already solved.

## While you work

- **Start with the simplest thing that could work.** Simple baselines told me more than clever ideas, and they gave me something to beat.
- **Keep a log, and keep it daily.** Short entries are fine. What you tried, what happened, and what you'd try next. Future you will need it.
- **Save your failures.** I wrote down ideas that didn't work, along with why I thought they wouldn't. It stopped me and others from trying them twice.
- **Compare like with like.** When two approaches were measured under different conditions, I said so plainly instead of putting them in a table as if they were equal.
- **Look at the raw data yourself.** A few minutes spent flipping through examples answered questions that hours of metrics never did.

## With people

- **Use one-on-ones.** They are where I found out what mattered to the team, and where a single piece of feedback could redirect a whole project.
- **Say what you don't know.** Nobody expects an intern to know everything, and everybody notices whether you can explain what you have and haven't verified.
- **Help test things.** Trying out a teammate's tool taught me how easily software breaks in the hands of someone who didn't write it, which made me write more careful tools of my own.

## At the end

- **Write it up for a stranger.** The best measure of whether your work will survive after you leave is whether someone with no context can pick it up.
- **Make it fit.** If it only works on your laptop, it isn't finished. Getting things running inside the team's real setup was part of the job.
- **Prepare a talk for a mixed audience.** I built my final presentation for people from different backgrounds, which forced me to be clear about the problem before showing any solution.
- **Say thank you.** People spend real time teaching interns. I try to make sure they know it mattered.
