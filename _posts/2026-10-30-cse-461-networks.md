Before [CSE 461 Lab 1](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse461/26au/lab/lab1.html), I could explain the difference between UDP and TCP in a sentence. Building a client and server around a multi-stage protocol made me appreciate how much that sentence leaves out.

The lab's conversation progresses through four stages. The client starts with a UDP greeting and receives parameters for the next step. It then sends a sequence of UDP packets, waits for acknowledgments, and moves to TCP for the remaining exchange. Values returned in one stage become inputs to the next. I liked that structure: each successful exchange unlocks the next, but it also means that a small misunderstanding early on can show up much later.

## The details between the endpoints

A packet isn't just the message I want to send. I had to encode a header with lengths and other fields in network byte order, distinguish the payload length from the padding on the wire, and check what came back. Looking at the bytes made the protocol feel less like a diagram and more like an agreement: both programs need to interpret the same sequence in the same way.

UDP made me think about what happens when a response doesn't arrive. My client sends a packet, waits for its acknowledgment, and retries after a timeout. I also had to consider the final result arriving before the last acknowledgment. The logic felt straightforward when I drew it out, but spelling out those cases in code showed me how quickly a conversation becomes a state machine.

TCP gave me a different problem. It delivers a stream, so one receive call isn't guaranteed to return a whole header or payload. I wrote an exact-read loop that accumulates bytes and handles a closed connection. That was a useful reminder that reliability at one layer does not remove the need to define message boundaries at another.

## What I can actually claim

My local simulated checks passed, but the environment I used could not bind sockets or reach the course server. I still need to check interoperability in a network-capable environment. I care about that distinction because a test can give me confidence in a piece of behavior without proving the whole conversation works in its intended setting.

The experience made me more patient with details that are easy to dismiss as plumbing. When two independent programs communicate, the exact bytes, timeouts, and assumptions *are* the interface. I now notice those decisions much more when I use a networked application.
